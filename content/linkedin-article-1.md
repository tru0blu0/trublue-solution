## Draft

Most businesses don't have an operations problem. They have an architecture problem.

Here's what I mean:

When I walk into a company that's "struggling with operations," I see the same pattern every time. The founder built the business around themselves. Every process depends on them knowing something, deciding something, or remembering something. The business isn't a system — it's a person with employees.

That works at $500K. It cracks at $2M. It breaks catastrophically at $5M.

The fix isn't better employees. It's not more meetings. It's not a new piece of software.

The fix is operational architecture: documented processes that survive staff turnover, measurement systems that give you visibility without you asking, and workflows designed for the people who run them, not the person who designed them.

I spent 16 years building this kind of infrastructure from the inside — P&Ls, SOPs, compliance programs, TMS deployments, vendor controls — across logistics, restaurants, legal, and IT. Same problems, different industries. Same root cause.

Three signs your operation has an architecture problem:

1. **You can't take a vacation.** If the business breaks when you're gone, you don't have systems. You have a job.

2. **You know something's wrong but can't prove it.** If you can't put a number on your COGS, your vendor spend, or your process bottlenecks, you're flying blind.

3. **Every fix creates two new problems.** If you're constantly putting out fires, you're treating symptoms. The structure is the problem.

The businesses that scale aren't the ones with the best ideas. They're the ones with the best operational infrastructure. Ideas are cheap. Systems are expensive — but only if you don't build them.
